Introduction: what is probability?

Classical probability, as stated by Laplace, defines the probability of an event as the ratio of favourable to total equally likely outcomes.

$$ P(A)=\frac{\text{\# ways of happening A}}{\text{\# total of possible events}} $$

Example: Rolling a dice: $$ \Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\} \Rightarrow P(A=i)=\frac{1}{6},\quad i \in{1,\ldots,6} $$

Introduction: what is probability?

Classical probability, as stated by Laplace, defines the probability of an event as the ratio of favourable to total equally likely outcomes.

$$ P(A)=\frac{\text{\# ways of happening A}}{\text{\# total of possible events}} $$

 

Not adequate for infinite sample spaces and continuous quantities!

Introduction: what is probability?

The resolution is due to Kolmogorov that identified probability with a measure on a suitable collection of subsets of a sample space.

 

$$ P(A)=\int_A d\mu (A) $$ Probability measures the likelihood of some random event happening

Samples Spaces

Definition: A sample space $\Omega$ is a non-empty set whose elements $\omega\in\Omega$ are called outcomes (or sample points). A subset $A\subseteq\Omega$ that we wish to assign a probability is called an event. When $\omega\in A$ we say the event $A$ occurs.

Example: a dice
$$ \Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\} $$

Set operations

Set operations translate directly into the language of events:

 

  • $A\cup B$ is "$A$ or $B$";
  • $A\cap B$ is "$A$ and $B$";
  • $A^c=\Omega\setminus A$ is "not $A$";
  • $A\cap B=\varnothing$ means the events are mutually exclusive.

$\sigma$-Algebras

Definition: A collection $\mathcal{F}$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is a $\sigma$-algebra (or $\sigma$-field) if
  • $\Omega \in\mathcal{F}$;
  • $A\in\mathcal{F} \implies A^c\in\mathcal{F}$;
  • $A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal{F} \implies \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\in\mathcal{F}$.

The pair $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ is called a measurable space and the members of $\mathcal{F}$ are the measurable sets (events).

Borel $\sigma$-Algebras

Definition: The Borel $\sigma$-algebra on $\R$ is $\mathcal{B}(\R)=\sigma(\{(a,b):a < b\})$, the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the open intervals. Its members are the Borel sets. Equivalently one may generate $\mathcal{B}(\R)$ from the half-lines $\{(-\infty,x]:x\in\R\}$; this equivalence underlies the use of distribution functions.

Measure

Definition: Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ be a measurable space. A measure is a function $\mu:\Fscr\to[0,\infty]$ such that
  • $\mu(\varnothing)=0$;
  • for any sequence $\{A_n\}_{n\ge1}$ of pairwise disjoint sets in $\Fscr$, \[ \mu\!\Big(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\Big) =\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_n). \]

Probability Measure

 

Definition: Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ be a measurable space. A measure $\mu:\Fscr\to[0,\infty]$ such that $\mu(\Omega)=1$ is called a probability measure and is usually denoted $\mathbb{P}$.

Kolmogorov's Axioms

Kolmogorov's Axioms: A probability measure $\Prob$ on $(\Omega,\Fscr)$ satisfies \[ \begin{align*} \text{(P1)}\ \ & \Prob(A)\ge 0,\\ \text{(P2)}\ \ & \Prob(\Om)=1,\\ \text{(P3)}\ \ & \Prob\!\Big(\bigsqcup_{n}A_n\Big)=\sum_{n}\Prob(A_n), \text{for pairwise disjoint } A_n. \end{align*} \] The triple $(\Om,\Fscr,\Prob)$ is a probability space.

Consequences of Kolmogorov's Axioms

Proposition: Let $(\Om,\Fscr,\Prob)$ be a probability space and $A,B\in\Fscr$. Then $$ \begin{align} \Prob(A^c) &= 1-\Prob(A), \\ \Prob(\varnothing) &= 0, \\ A\subseteq B &\implies \Prob(A)\le \Prob(B), \\ \Prob(A\cup B) &= \Prob(A)+\Prob(B)-\Prob(A\cap B). \end{align} $$

Multiplication Rule

Proposition: For events $A_1,\dots,A_n$ with $\Prob(A_1\cap\dots\cap A_{n-1})>0$, \[ \Prob\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_i\Big)\\ =\Prob(A_1)\,\Prob(A_2\mid A_1)\,\Prob(A_3\mid A_1\cap A_2) \cdots \Prob\Big(A_n\ \Big|\ \bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1}A_i\Big). \]

Independence

Definition: Events $A,B$ are independent if $$\Prob(A\cap B)=\Prob(A)\,\Prob(B).$$ A family $\{A_i\}_{i\in I}$ is (mutually) independent if for every finite subset $J\subseteq I$, $$\Prob(\bigcap_{i\in J}A_i)=\prod_{i\in J}\Prob(A_i).$$

Independence of R. V.

Definition: Random variables $X,Y$ are independent if $$\Prob_{(X,Y)}=\Prob_X\otimes\Prob_Y.$$ For densities this reads $$f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y).$$

The Law of Total Probability

Definition: A countable family $\{B_1,B_2,\dots\}\subseteq\Fscr$ is a partition of $\Om$ if the $B_i$ are pairwise disjoint and $\bigcup_i B_i=\Om$.

The Law of Total Probability

Theorem: Let $\{B_i\}$ be a partition of $\Om$ with $\Prob(B_i)>0$ for all $i$. Then for every event $A$, \[ \Prob(A)=\sum_{i}\Prob(A\mid B_i)\,\Prob(B_i). \]

Conditional Probability

Definition: Let $B\in\Fscr$ with $\Prob(B)>0$. The conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is \[ \Prob(A\mid B)=\frac{\Prob(A\cap B)}{\Prob(B)}. \]
For fixed $B$ with $\Prob(B)>0$, the map $A\mapsto\Prob(A\mid B)$ is a probability measure on $(\Om,\Fscr)$!

The Bayesian Rule for Two Events

Given two events $A,B\in\Omega$, we have
\[ \Prob(A|B)=\frac{\Prob(B|A)\Prob(A)}{\Prob(B)} \]
Example:
Probability of raining today given that rained yesterday? $$ A=\{\text{raining today}\}\\ B=\{\text{rained yesterday}\} $$

The Bayesian Vocabulary

With hypothesis $B$ and data $A$, \[ \underbrace{\Prob(B\mid A)}_{\text{posterior}} \ = \frac{\underbrace{\Prob(A\mid B)}_{\text{likelihood}}\ \cdot\ \underbrace{\Prob(B)}_{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{P(A)}_{\text{evidence}}} , \]

The Bayesian Rule for Three Events

Given three events $A,B,C\in\Omega$, we have
\[ \Prob(A|B,C)=\frac{\Prob(B|A,C)\Prob(A|C)}{\Prob(B|C)} \]
Probability of raining today given that rained yesterday and it is summer? $$ A=\{\text{raining today}\}\\ B=\{\text{rained yesterday}\}\\ C=\{\text{it is summer}\} $$

The Bayes' Rule

Theorem: Let $\{B_i\}$ be a partition of $\Om$ with $\Prob(B_i)>0$, and let $A$ be an event with $\Prob(A)>0$. Then \[ \boxed{\ \Prob(B_k\mid A) =\frac{\Prob(A\mid B_k)\,\Prob(B_k)} {\displaystyle\sum_i \Prob(A\mid B_i)\,\Prob(B_i)}\ }. \]

Random Variables

Definition: Let $(\Om,\Fscr,\Prob)$ be a probability space. A function $X:\Om\to\R$ is a random variable if it is $\Fscr$-measurable, meaning $$ X^{-1}(B)=\{\om\in\Om : X(\om)\in B\}\in\Fscr \\\text{for every } B\in\Bscr(\R). $$

Intuition behind a Random Variables

A Random Variable (R.V.) $X$ is a function that associates a real number for each outcome of a random experiment

Expectation (Lebesgue Integral)

Theorem: For a measurable $g:\R\to\R$ with $\E|g(X)|<\infty$, \[ \E[g(X)]=\int_{\R}g(x)\dif\Prob_X(x) \]
Here, $$ \Prob_X(B) = \Prob (X\in B) (\text{distribution}) $$

Expectation (Lebesgue Integral)

Theorem: For a measurable $g:\R\to\R$ with $\E|g(X)|<\infty$, \[ \E[g(X)] =\begin{cases} \displaystyle\sum_i g(x_i)\,p_X(x_i), & X\text{ discrete},\\[8pt] \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)\,f_X(x)\dif x, & X\text{ abs.\ continuous.} \end{cases} \]

Mean and Variance

Mean: \[ \E[X]=\int_{\Om}X\dif\Prob_X \]
Variance: \[ \Var(X)=\E[(X-\E[X])^2]=\E[X^2]-(\E[X])^2\ge 0 \]

The Sampled Mean and Variance

Mean: \[ \E[X]=\int_{\Om}X\dif\Prob=\lim_{N\to \infty} \frac{1}{N}\sum_iX_i=\mu \]
Variance: \[ \Var(X)=\E[(X-\E[X])^2]=\lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N}(X_i-\mu)^2 \]

Expectation Properties

Theorem: For integrable $X,Y$ and $a,b\in\R$, we have \[ \E[aX+bY]= a\E[X]+b\E[y] (\text{linearity}) \] and \[ X\le Y \Rightarrow \E[X]\le E[Y] (\text{monotonicity}) \]
In addition, if $X$ and $Y$ are independent then $$ \E[XY]=\E[X]\E[Y] $$

Covariance

Theorem: For integrable $X,Y$, we have \[ \text{Cov}(X,Y)= \E[(X-\E[X])(Y-\E[Y])] \]
Note that, $$ \text{Cov}(X,Y)= \E[XY]-\E[X]\E[Y] $$ Therefore, if $X$ and $Y$ are independent we have $$ \text{Cov}(X,Y)=0. $$

Covariance

Covariance vs Variance

Theorem: For integrable $X$, we have \[ \text{Var}(X)= \Cov(X,X) \]
Moreover, $$ \text{Var}(X+Y)= \Var(X)+\Var(Y)+2\Cov(X,Y) $$ Therefore, if $X$ and $Y$ are independent we have $$ \text{Var}(X+Y)=\Var(X)+\Var(Y). $$

Correlation

\[ \Corr(X,Y)=\frac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\Var(X)\Var(Y)}} \]
In this way, we have $$ -1\le \Corr(X,Y) \le 1 $$ Also, we can show that $$ \Corr(aX+b,cY+d)=\Corr(X,Y) $$

Random Variables in $\R^n$

Let $\vec{X}\in\R^n$, then the density of $\vec{X}$ is
\[ \Prob_{\vec{X}}(B)=\Prob(\vec{X}\in B) \]
Accordingly, the expectation is $$ \E[\vec{X}]=\int_{\Omega \subseteq \R^n} \vec{X} d\Prob_{\vec{X}} $$

Gaussian Random Variables in $\R^n$

Definition: A R.V. $\vec{X}\in\R^n$ is called Gaussian Distributed if $$ \Prob_{\vec{X}}(A)=\int_A \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^n |\Sigma|}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\|X-\mu\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^2\Bigr)d\vec{X} $$
Here, $$ \begin{align*} \|X-\mu\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^2&=(X-\mu)^\trp \Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)\\ \mu&=\E[\vec{X}]\\ \Sigma &= \Cov(\vec{X}) \end{align*} $$
