Probabilistic State Space Model

Definition: A probabilistic state space model is defined as $$ \begin{aligned} x_{k}&\sim p(x_{k}|x_{k-1})\rightarrow \text{dynamic model}\\ y_{k}&\sim p(y_k|x_k)\rightarrow \text{measurement model} \end{aligned} $$ Hypothesis: $$ \begin{aligned} p(x_{k}|x_{0:k-1},y_{1:k-1})&=p(x_{k}|x_{k-1}) \text{(Markov Property)}\\ p(y_k|x_{0:k},y_{1:k-1})&=p(y_k|x_k) \text{(Conditional Independence)} \end{aligned} $$

Bayesian Filtering

The purpose of Bayesian filtering is to obtain the marginal posterior distribution $$ \begin{aligned} p(x_{k}|y_{1:k}) \end{aligned} $$ recursively for each time step $k=1,2,\ldots$

Bayesian Filtering

Theorem
  • Initialization: starts with a prior distribution $p(x_0)$;
  • Prediction step (Chapman-Kolmogorov Eq.): $$ p(x_{k}|y_{1:k-1})=\int p(x_{k}|x_{k-1})p(x_{k-1}|y_{1:k-1})dx_{k-1} $$
  • Update step (Bayes' rule): $$ p(x_{k}|y_{1:k})=\frac{p(y_{k}|x_{k})p(x_{k}|y_{1:k-1})}{p(y_{k}|y_{1:k-1})} $$

Suppose we want to solve the following multidimensional integral
$$ I=\int g(x)dx $$
where $g: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ is some complicated function that cannot be integrated analytically.

Then, the integral can be restated as
$$ I=\int f(x)\pi(x)dx $$

Monte Carlo Integration

Assume that $g(x)=f(x)\pi(x)$, where $\pi: \mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is interpreted as a probability density satisfying
$$ \pi(x)\ge 0,\quad \int \pi(x)dx = 1 $$
Then, the integral can be approximated by
$$ \hat{I}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^Nf(x^{(i)}),\quad x^{(i)}\sim\pi(x), \quad N<\infty $$

Monte Carlo Integration (MC)

$$ \hat{I}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^Nf(x^{(i)}),\quad x^{(i)}\sim\pi(x), \quad N<\infty $$

Observations:

  • The samples concentrate in regions where $\pi(x)$ is large;
  • The error decreases as $\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})$, a rate independent of the dimension;
  • Usually we cannot sample directly from $\pi(x)$ (e.g., a posterior known only up to a normalizing constant).

Suppose we can only generate samples from a density $q(x)$ that is similar to $\pi(x)$. Can we still make the MC estimation effective?

Answer: yes! If $q(x)>0$ wherever $f(x)\pi(x)\neq 0$ (support condition), then

$$ I=\int f(x)\pi(x)dx = \int f(x)\frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)}q(x)dx $$
The PDF $q(x)$ is referred to as the importance or proposal density.

Sequential Importance Sampling (SIS)

It is a technique for implementing recursive Bayesian filters using Monte Carlo simulations!

 

Advantage: As the number of samples becomes larger, the SIS filter approaches the optimal Bayesian estimator!

Sequential Importance Sampling (SIS)

The key idea is to represent the required posterior density function by a set of random samples with associated weights.
$$ p(x_{k}|y_{1:k})\approx \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \delta(x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k}^{(i)}) $$
  • Applies to non-Gaussian distributions;
  • Can represent multimodal distributions;
  • Handles mixed continuous–discrete states;
  • Theoretically optimal for $N\to\infty$.

The Optimal Proposal Distribution

For Markov models it is natural to take $q(x_{k}|x_{0:k-1},y_{1:k})=q(x_{k}|x_{k-1},y_{k})$. It can be proven that the optimal proposal distribution (in the sense of minimum weight variance) is $$ \boxed{q^*(x_{k}|x_{k-1}^{(i)},y_{k})=p(x_{k}|x_{k-1}^{(i)},y_{k})} $$ which gives $w_{k}^{(i)}\propto w_{k-1}^{(i)}\, p(y_{k}|x_{k-1}^{(i)})$. In general, it cannot be sampled from exactly.

SIS Algorithm

  • Initialization: Draw $N$ samples $\hat{x}_0^{(i)}$ from the initial distribution: $$ \boxed{\hat{x}_0^{(i)} \sim p(x_0),\quad i=1,\ldots, N} $$ and set $w_0^{(i)}=\frac{1}{N}$ for all $i=1,\ldots,N$.

SIS Algorithm

  • Time Update: For all $k=1,2,\ldots$ do the following:
    1. Draw samples $\hat{x}_k^{(i)}$ from the proposal distribution: $$ \boxed{\hat{x}_k^{(i)} \sim q(x_k|\hat{x}_{0:k-1}^{(i)},y_k),\quad i=1,\ldots,N} $$
    2. Compute the weights according to $$ \boxed{w_k^{(i)} \propto w_{k-1}^{(i)} \frac{p(y_k|\hat{x}_k^{(i)})p(\hat{x}_{k}^{(i)}|\hat{x}_{k-1}^{(i)})}{q(\hat{x}_k^{(i)}|\hat{x}_{0:k-1}^{(i)},y_k)}} $$ and normalize them to sum to unity.

SIS Algorithm

Mean and Covariance:
$$ \begin{aligned} \hat{x}_{k|k}&=\sum_{i=1}^N w_k^{(i)} \hat{x}_k^{(i)} \\ P_{k|k} & = \sum_{i=1}^N w_k^{(i)} (\hat{x}_k^{(i)}-\hat{x}_{k|k})(\bullet)^\trp \end{aligned} $$
for all $k=1,2,\ldots$

Particle Filter Workflow

Degeneracy Problem

One problem in SIS is that, after a few iterations, all but one (or a few) particles have negligible weight. This is the so-called degeneracy problem.

Resampling

Replaces the set of particles with a new one drawn according to the discrete distribution defined by the weights.
The resampling procedure can be described as follows:
  • Interpret each weight $w_k^{(i)}$ as the probability of obtaining the sample index $i$ in the set $\{\hat{x}_k^{(i)} : i = 1, \dots, N\}$;
  • Draw $N$ samples from that discrete distribution and replace the old sample set with this new one;
  • Set all weights to the constant value $w_k^{(i)} = 1/N$.

Sequential Importance Resampling (SIR)

Combining SIS with resampling results in the so-called Sequential Importance Resampling (SIR), which is the basis for most particle filter implementations.
Usual condition for resampling (estimated effective sample size): $$ \boxed{\hat{N}_\text{eff}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^N (w_k^{(i)})^2}} $$ Resample if $ \hat{N}_\text{eff} < \eta $ where $\eta$ is a threshold, typically $\eta = N/2$.

Bootstrap Particle Filter

Assume that $$ q(x_{k}|x_{k-1},y_{k})=p(x_{k}|x_{k-1}) $$
and resample at every time step.
Accordingly, $$ \boxed{w_{k}^{(i)}\propto w_{k-1}^{(i)} \frac{p(y_{k}|\hat{x}_{k}^{(i)})p(\hat{x}_{k}^{(i)}|\hat{x}_{k-1}^{(i)})}{q(\hat{x}_{k}^{(i)}|\hat{x}_{0:k-1}^{(i)},y_{1:k})}} $$

Bootstrap Particle Filter

Assume that $$ q(x_{k}|x_{k-1},y_{k})=p(x_{k}|x_{k-1}) $$
Accordingly, $$ \boxed{w_{k}^{(i)}\propto p(y_{k}|\hat{x}_{k}^{(i)})} $$

Bootstrap PF Algorithm

  • Initialization: Draw $N$ samples $\hat{x}_0^{(i)}$ from the initial distribution: $$ \boxed{\hat{x}_0^{(i)} \sim p(x_0),\quad w_0^{(i)}=\frac{1}{N},\quad i=1,\ldots, N} $$
  • Time Update: For all $k=1,2,\ldots$ do the following:
    Draw samples $\hat{x}_k^{(i)}$ from the state transition distribution: $$ \boxed{\hat{x}_k^{(i)} \sim p(x_k|\hat{x}_{k-1}^{(i)}),\quad i=1,\ldots,N} $$

Bootstrap PF Algorithm

  • Compute the weights according to $$ \boxed{w_k^{(i)} \propto p(y_k|\hat{x}_k^{(i)})} $$ and normalize them to sum to unity.
  • Compute the mean and covariance: $$ \boxed{\hat{x}_{k|k}=\sum_{i=1}^N w_k^{(i)} \hat{x}_k^{(i)}} ,\quad \boxed{P_{k|k} = \sum_{i=1}^N w_k^{(i)} (\hat{x}_k^{(i)}-\hat{x}_{k|k})(\bullet)^\trp} $$
  • Do resampling.

Local Linearization Particle Filter (LLPF)

Assume that
$$ q(x_{k}|\hat{x}_{0:k-1}^{(i)},y_{k})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}^{(i)}_{k|k},P_{k|k}^{(i)}) $$
where $\{\hat{x}_{k|k}^{(i)},P_{k|k}^{(i)}\}$ is obtained, for each particle, by one prediction and update step (using $y_{k}$) of a nonlinear Kalman filter (EKF or UKF) started at $\hat{x}_{k-1}^{(i)}$.
  • EKF → Extended Kalman Particle Filter (EKPF);
  • UKF → Unscented Particle Filter (UPF).

Particle Filter

Observations:
  • The local linearization method approximates the optimal importance density and moves particles toward regions of high likelihood;
  • Since particle filters are computationally expensive, one should use them only when conventional Kalman filters (KF, EKF, UKF) do not produce satisfactory results.
  • Resampling duplicates high-weight particles, so the set may collapse to a few distinct values (sample impoverishment), especially when the process noise is small.
